AI-native talent becomes meaningful in the context of the work. The starting question is what the business needs someone to build or change, and how AI changes the capability required. A research role, an AI product role, and an engineering role using AI extensively need different evidence.

This is how I approach the search and the conversations that follow.

## Define what AI-native means for this role

The hiring leader and I need a shared view of the contribution: the problem, the user, the technical constraints, and the decisions the person will own. That helps distinguish where deep model expertise matters, where product or engineering judgment matters, and where someone needs to learn quickly across disciplines.

The environment matters too. Access to data, specialist colleagues, evaluation infrastructure, and decision authority shapes what someone can achieve. Those conditions belong in the success profile and in the candidate conversation.

## Look for relevant work and follow the contribution

My search approach maps the problem to organisations, adjacent talent pools, and people doing relevant work. For AI-native talent, useful starting points can include shipped products, open-source contributions, technical writing, research, and accounts of how a system was built or improved.

Each is a lead to examine. Public visibility is uneven; confidential work may leave little public trace. The conversation needs to establish what the person actually owned, which constraints they faced, and what evidence they can appropriately share.

[Sourcing Compass](../projects/sourcing-compass/) reflects the broader search discipline: making target organisations, adjacent pools, and related titles visible before outreach begins.

## Follow the proof from launch to learning

For a role that requires production AI experience, I want to understand one system in depth. The evidence needs to connect:

- **Use:** who relied on it and what work it helped them do.
- **Ownership:** what the person designed, built, or decided themselves.
- **Operation:** what failed after launch, how they diagnosed it, and what they changed.
- **Measurement:** how they knew it worked, what they compared it with, and what the measure missed.
- **Constraints:** how data access, security, customer requirements, cost, or reliability changed the design.

The role determines which evidence is essential and how current the experience needs to be. Using AI tools is useful context; a production-building role needs evidence of building and operating the system itself. Specialist depth needs the right specialist assessor.

A precise account of a failure can be particularly useful. It connects personal responsibility, diagnosis, and learning in a way that a polished demonstration alone cannot establish.

## Look for a reasoned view that can evolve

Coachability matters alongside the capability needed today. I look for someone who can explain a decision, examine a challenge to it, and revise their view when better evidence changes the answer. They should also be able to explain why an objection does not change their reasoning.

An interview can explore that through a real design decision: what information was available, what would have changed the choice, and how the person responds to a relevant new constraint. The useful evidence is the reasoning and the change it produces.

That opens a second question: how will this person grow as the work changes? The immediate requirements should be clear and limited to what the role truly needs. Learning, judgment under ambiguity, and the ability to improve the team’s thinking deserve their own examination.

## Write the standard before judging the candidate

The hiring team needs a shared, written definition of the evidence required before interviews begin. Each part of the interview should have a purpose and an owner. Notes should connect the conclusion to what the candidate actually said or demonstrated.

Missing evidence needs to remain visible. Record what is unconfirmed and agree on a focused follow-up, instead of silently treating an unanswered question as demonstrated inability. If the standard changes during a search, record the change and revisit affected assessments.

This is part of how I build [interview frameworks](../advisory/#hiring-and-interview-architecture): a standard the team can apply, question, and improve through the work.

## Make the opportunity worth exploring

Strong outreach connects a person’s work with a real problem they might want to own. The conversation should make the scope, colleagues, constraints, and room to learn concrete. It should also give the candidate a way to test those claims with the people they would work alongside.

That is part of the wider [Art & Craft of Recruiting](../recruiting-craft/): understanding the business, assessing contribution, advising the hiring team, and helping both sides make an informed choice.

## AI in the recruiting workflow

Finding AI-native talent and building AI-enabled recruiting systems are connected areas of my work. [Interview Evaluator](../projects/gabbar-interview-evaluator/) supports interviewer development; [RishiOS](../projects/rishios-mcp/) connects hiring standards, evidence, and calibration. The people responsible for a hire remain accountable for the decision.

Research and synthesis need source checks. AI-generated claims about a candidate cannot establish their capability. This discipline also runs through *[Raising the Bar](../raising-the-bar/)*.
