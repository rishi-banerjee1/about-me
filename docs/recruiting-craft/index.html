Understand the work before defining the hire

A search starts with what the business needs this person to change. I work with the hiring leader to clarify the decisions they will own, the constraints they will inherit, and what a successful first year would look like. That conversation shapes the level, scope, and evidence we should pursue.

This is also where I test the brief. Which requirements are essential to doing the work? Which can be learned? Where do scope, compensation, location, and market availability pull in different directions? Making those trade-offs explicit gives the hiring team a standard it can use when real candidates arrive.

Find relevant talent and earn the conversation

My search experience includes retained and contingent mandates across North America in technology, advisory, engineering, and business functions. The craft is in recognising where someone has solved a relevant problem, including when their title or industry makes that experience less obvious.

I map target organisations, adjacent pools, and variations in role scope, then test those assumptions through research and conversations. Outreach needs a credible connection between the person's work and the opportunity: why their experience matters, what they could own, and why a conversation is worth their time. What I learn from the market changes where I look next.

I built Sourcing Compass to make that early reasoning visible: target companies, adjacent pools, less obvious sources, and related titles. It gives a recruiter and hiring leader something concrete to examine together before outreach begins.

Understand how ability becomes impact

I look closely at how someone applies their skills: the problem they chose to solve, their own contribution, the decisions they made under constraints, and what happened as a result. Those details help me understand both capability and the conditions in which it will translate to this role.

The same discipline matters in a debrief. I ask what observation supports a judgment, where interviewers have conflicting evidence, and what we still need to learn. A concern should be specific enough to investigate; a recommendation should make its reasoning visible.

Interview Evaluator tool reflects that approach through structured evidence and potential-bias flags. It supports the assessment conversation. Accountability for interpreting the evidence and making the hiring decision stays with people.

Be the advisor who keeps the search moving

Hiring leaders need counsel they can act on. I bring a recommendation, the evidence behind it, the trade-off it creates, and the decision needed to move forward. That can mean revisiting a must-have after market feedback, sharpening what an interview should test, or resolving disagreement about a candidate.

When a search stalls, I trace where it loses momentum. A sourcing constraint, an unclear role, weak assessment signals, and delayed decisions each call for a different response. I work through that diagnosis with the hiring leader and coach interviewers in the context of the candidates in front of us. The partnership gets stronger as we develop a shared understanding of the role and the evidence.

Help a candidate make a decision they believe in

Closing starts with understanding the person: what they want to build, what they need from their next environment, and what gives them pause. I carry that context through the search, prepare them for meaningful conversations, and help the hiring team address the questions that will shape their decision.

That includes being clear about the work, the expectations, and the uncertainties. By the offer stage, both sides should understand why the match makes sense and which questions remain open.

I built Atlan Offer Wizard to carry that understanding into the offer itself: what stood out about the person, their connection to the company, and the opportunity ahead. The artifact makes the final communication personal and grounded in the conversations that came before it.

Develop teams through the work

I coach recruiters, hiring leaders, and interviewers through the decisions a live search demands: how they frame the role, explain the market, distinguish an assessment claim from its evidence, and advise the hiring leader. I ask them to bring a recommendation, make their reasoning clear, and own the next action.

The aim is growing independence in judgment. A Talent Partner should be able to recognise when the brief needs challenging, when the search needs a different direction, and when a candidate needs a more honest conversation. Reviewing those moments together turns experience into capability the recruiter can carry into the next search.

I have written more about this practice in Raising the Bar.
